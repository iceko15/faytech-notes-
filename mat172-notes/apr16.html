Conic Sections: Graphing & Equation Construction

BIG IDEA

Conic sections (ellipses, hyperbolas, and parabolas) share similar structural components in their equations but possess distinct geometric properties. The transition from General Form to Standard Form via completing the square is essential for identifying landmarks like centers, vertices, foci, and directrices. Constructing equations from given properties requires a visual approach to determine orientation (vertical vs. horizontal).

Exam Hint: Identifying shapes from General Form is a common final exam question. You don't need to put the equation in standard form just to name the shape; look at the coefficients of the $x^2$ and $y^2$ terms.

KEY TERMS

  • Standard Form: The equation format that explicitly reveals the center $(h, k)$ and distances $a$ and $b$.
  • Foci (Focal Points): Landmark points used to establish the graph. In physics/science, these are where light or energy might concentrate (e.g., reflecting off a parabola).
  • Major Axis (Ellipse): The longer axis; its orientation is determined by the larger denominator.
  • Transverse Axis (Hyperbola): The axis connecting the vertices; its orientation is determined by which variable is positive.
  • Latus Rectum (Parabola): The segment through the focus, perpendicular to the axis of symmetry, with length $|4p|$.
  • Directrix (Parabola): A fixed line outside the curve; the distance from the vertex to the directrix is equal to the distance from the vertex to the focus ($|p|$).

STEPS/PROCESS

1. Identifying the Shape from General Form

Scenario Resulting Shape
Missing a square ($x^2$ or $y^2$ is gone) Parabola
Both squared, same coefficients, same sign Circle
Both squared, different coefficients, same sign Ellipse
Both squared, different signs (one is minus) Hyperbola

2. Completing the Square for Parabolas

Step 1:

Move all terms with the squared variable to one side; move everything else to the other.

Step 2:

Use the formula $(\frac{b}{2})^2$ to find the constant needed to complete the square.

Step 3:

Add that constant to both sides to maintain balance.

Step 4:

Factor the squared side and factor out the coefficient of the non-squared variable on the other side to reveal $4p$.
MyMathLab Warning: When graphing parabolas in the software, you must plot the vertex first and then specifically a Latus Rectum endpoint. It often will not accept an x or y intercept as the second point.

RULES/IDENTITIES

Focal Distance Formulas

  • Ellipse: $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$ (The middle sign is the opposite of the standard form's plus).
  • Hyperbola: $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$ (The middle sign is the opposite of the standard form's minus).
  • Parabola: Focal distance is $p$, found by solving $4p = [\text{coefficient}]$.

EXAMPLE 1: Hyperbola Center at $(h, k)$

Equation: $\frac{(y+2)^2}{4} - \frac{(x-1)^2}{16} = 1$
  • Center: $(1, -2)$. Notice the $y$ is first in the equation, but $h$ stays with $x$ and $k$ stays with $y$! Don't reverse them out of habit.
  • Orientation: Opens Up/Down because $y^2$ is the positive term.
  • Distances: $a^2=4 \rightarrow a=2$ (vertical); $b^2=16 \rightarrow b=4$ (horizontal).
  • Vertices: $(1, 0)$ and $(1, -4)$ (Move 2 units up/down from center).
  • Asymptotes: Formed by the box that is 4 units wide and 2 units tall from the center.
  • Foci Calculation: $c^2 = 4 + 16 = 20 \rightarrow c = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5} \approx 4.5$.
  • Foci Points: $(1, -2 + 2\sqrt{5})$ and $(1, -2 - 2\sqrt{5})$.

EXAMPLE 2: Parabola via Completing the Square

Original: $y^2 - 2y + 12x - 35 = 0$

1. Isolate $y$ terms:

$y^2 - 2y = -12x + 35$

2. Complete the square:

$(\frac{-2}{2})^2 = 1$. Add 1 to both sides:
$(y^2 - 2y + 1) = -12x + 35 + 1$

3. Factor:

$(y - 1)^2 = -12x + 36$

4. Factor out coefficient:

$(y - 1)^2 = -12(x - 3)$
  • Vertex: $(3, 1)$.
  • Orientation: $y$ is squared (Left/Right) and $4p$ is negative (Left).
  • P-value: $4p = -12 \rightarrow p = -3$.
  • Focus: $(0, 1)$ (3 units left of vertex).
  • Directrix: $x = 6$ (3 units right of vertex).
  • Latus Rectum: $|4p| = 12$. (Count 6 units up and 6 units down from the focus to find endpoints).

EXAMPLE 3: Writing Ellipse Equation from Points

Given: Foci at $(0, \pm4)$; Vertices at $(0, \pm7)$.
  • Center: Midpoint is $(0, 0)$.
  • Orientation: Vertical (Major axis is on the y-axis).
  • Identify $a$ and $c$: $a = 7$ (distance to vertex); $c = 4$ (distance to focus).
  • Find $b$: $c^2 = a^2 - b^2 \rightarrow 16 = 49 - b^2 \rightarrow b^2 = 33$.
  • Equation: $\frac{x^2}{33} + \frac{y^2}{49} = 1$.

SUMMARY

  • Visual Aids: Always doodle the points. It prevents orientation errors and sign mistakes in $h$ and $k$.
  • Hyperbola vs. Ellipse: In hyperbolas, $a^2$ is always under the first (positive) term. In ellipses, $a^2$ is always the larger number.
  • Landmarks: Foci and vertices always lie on the same axis (Major or Transverse). Directrices are lines, while centers, vertices, and foci are points.
